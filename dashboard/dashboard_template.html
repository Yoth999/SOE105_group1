AI Revenue Forecast Experimental

Five forecasting models on the same 209 weeks of real sales-order history — four trained from scratch, plus our own Ensemble that averages them — each projecting Apr 2025 – Mar 2026 and each scored on the same held-out year. Switch between ฿ Revenue and Units — both are forecast separately, on the same data and the same protocol.

Ensemble — our own model, built on top of the other four. For every month it simply takes the plain average of the four forecasts, with no extra training and no weighting. Each model is wrong in a different direction, so averaging cancels part of that error — which is why on the held-out year it beats every single one of them.
LSTM — Long Short-Term Memory, a recurrent neural network. Reads the last 26 weeks in order and learns repeating shapes, so it is the only method here that can bend with the season instead of just following the level.
SARIMA — Seasonal AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average, the classical statistical forecaster. It differences the series to remove the trend, then models what is left as a mix of past values and past errors. Simple and hard to beat on short series, but it only sees the revenue line itself.
LightGBM — gradient boosted decision trees. Instead of reading the sequence it learns from engineered features (revenue 1–52 weeks ago, 4- and 12-week averages, week of year), then adds hundreds of small trees that each correct the previous error. Strong on tabular problems, but trees cannot extrapolate beyond values they have seen.
XGBoost — extreme gradient boosting. Also a forest of corrective trees, but each split is chosen by a regularised gain that trades accuracy against tree complexity, which keeps it steadier than plain boosting on small data.
Backtest — predicted vs actual, Apr 2024 – Mar 2025

Trained on Apr 2021 – Mar 2024 only, then asked to predict the year it had never seen — all five models over the same year. "Off %" is the average monthly error.

Frequently Bought Together

Product pairs customers repeatedly order in the same purchase — use these to suggest add-ons, build bundles, or prompt reorders

Always together every order containing one item also contained the other (100%)
Usually together 75–99% of those orders
Often together 60–74%
Sometimes together under 60% — worth knowing, but not a reliable bundle
The percentage is a share, not a count: "on 96% of ATA orders (27 of 28)" means ATA appeared on 28 sales orders and 27 of them also had the partner item. That is why two cards can both show 5 shared orders and still carry different labels — 5 out of 5 is "Always", 5 out of 8 is "Often". Hover a card for the reverse direction.
?Shared orders = sales orders that contained both items (minimum 5). The badge % = of all orders containing the stronger item, the share that also contained the other one — that share, not the count, sets the label. Cards are ranked by shared orders × that share, so frequent and consistent pairs come first.

Detailed Inventory Management Full Detail + Pricing

The complete item-by-item view — status, pricing, value tier, units shipped, volume tier, demand pattern, and recommended action for every product

Value Tier — by revenue contribution
High Value drives most of your revenue
Medium Value steady, moderate contributor
Low Value contributes little on its own
Demand Pattern — by predictability
Stable Demand consistent, easy to forecast
Variable Demand fluctuates, may need buffer stock
Irregular Demand sporadic, hard to forecast
Volume Tier — by units shipped
High Volume the SKUs that make up the top 80% of all pieces shipped
Medium Volume the next 15% of pieces
Low Volume the remaining 5% — moves rarely
Read Value Tier and Volume Tier together — they disagree more often than you would expect. A Low Value + High Volume item is cheap but fills the warehouse and ties up picking time, while High Value + Low Volume earns well on a handful of orders a year and rarely deserves standing stock. Demand Pattern then says how safely you can plan either one.
